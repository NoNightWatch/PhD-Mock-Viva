# Seat reservations and waiting time in a fictional library

This complete miniature paper is a fictional demonstration written for this repository. All observations and numbers are invented. It is not a real study, a research claim, or a validation dataset for the guide. Section numbers provide stable locations for the example questions.

## 1. Research question

Could an online reservation policy reduce waiting time for seats in a university library? The study aimed to assess the feasibility of introducing reservations and describe changes in waiting time. The initial expectation was that reservations would reduce waiting time and improve the library experience.

## 2. Design and measurements

One fictional library was observed for two consecutive teaching weeks. In week A, visitors joined a walk-in queue. In week B, an online reservation policy was introduced and opening hours increased by two hours each day. Assignment was not randomized, and no comparison library was included.

Waiting time was measured from joining the physical queue to entering a seat. Logs were collected from 120 completed visits in each week. People who left without obtaining a seat were not included. The study did not measure time spent booking online, unsuccessful booking attempts, accessibility barriers, or satisfaction. The two weeks could differ in demand and visitor composition. No individual-level dataset or uncertainty intervals are supplied in this miniature paper.

## 3. Descriptive findings

Median observed waiting time was 18 minutes in week A and 12 minutes in week B. The difference was 6 minutes, equivalent to one third of the week A median. These are descriptive summaries of the included completed visits; statistical significance was not assessed.

Staff were able to operate the booking system during the second week. That observation supports a limited feasibility statement. No evidence was collected about long-term maintenance costs or operation at other libraries.

## 4. Interpretation and limitations

The combined changes were accompanied by lower observed median waiting time. The design does not identify how much came from the reservation policy, longer opening hours, or other differences between the weeks. Excluding people who did not obtain a seat may also alter the interpretation of the waiting-time measure.

The findings support testing reservations further. The original expectation about an improved overall library experience remains untested because satisfaction, accessibility, and total user effort were not measured. A lower queue-waiting median is insufficient by itself to establish those outcomes.

## 5. Proposed next study

A follow-up could compare sites or periods under matched opening hours and explicitly examine variation in demand. It should record unsuccessful visits, time spent booking, accessibility, and satisfaction as well as queue waiting. The comparison strategy would need to account for differences between sites, changes over time, and visitors moving between libraries. These are proposals, not completed tests.
